\section{Mathematical Framework and Entanglement Measures}

\subsection{Hamiltonian and Symmetry.}
The Lipkin--Meshkov--Glick (LMG) model describes $N$ spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ particles interacting through collective spin operators.  
For four fermions, the case we consider in the examples  here, each levels has
degeneration $d=4$, leading to different total spin values.  The two
levels have quantum numbers $\sigma=\pm 1$, with the upper level
having $2\sigma=+1$ and energy $\varepsilon_{1}= \varepsilon/2$. The
lower level has $2\sigma=-1$ and energy
$\varepsilon_{2}=-\varepsilon/2$. That is, the lowest single-particle
level has negative spin projection (or spin down), while the upper
level has spin up.  In addition, the substates of each level are
characterized by the quantum numbers $p=1,2,3,4$.



The Hamiltonian of the system is given by
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\hat{H}=&\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{1}+\hat{H}_{2}\\
&\\
\hat{H}_{0}=&\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\sum_{\sigma ,p}\sigma
a_{\sigma,p}^{\dagger}a_{\sigma ,p}\\
&\\
\hat{H}_{1}=&\frac{1}{2}V\sum_{\sigma ,p,p'}
a_{\sigma,p}^{\dagger}a_{\sigma ,p'}^{\dagger}
a_{-\sigma ,p'}a_{-\sigma ,p}\\
&\\
\hat{H}_{2}=&\frac{1}{2}W\sum_{\sigma ,p,p'}
a_{\sigma,p}^{\dagger}a_{-\sigma ,p'}^{\dagger}
a_{\sigma ,p'}a_{-\sigma ,p}\\
&\\
\end{array}
\]
where $V$ and $W$ are constants. The operator 
$H_{1}$ can move pairs of fermions
while $H_{2}$ is a spin-exchange term. The latter
moves a pair of fermions from a state $(p\sigma ,p' -\sigma)$ to a state
$(p-\sigma ,p'\sigma)$.

We are going to rewrite the above Hamiltonian in terms of so-called  quasispin operators
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\hat{J}_{+}=&\sum_{p}
a_{p+}^{\dagger}a_{p-}\\
&\\
\hat{J}_{-}=&\sum_{p}
a_{p-}^{\dagger}a_{p+}\\
&\\
\hat{J}_{z}=&\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p\sigma}\sigma
a_{p\sigma}^{\dagger}a_{p\sigma}\\
&\\
\hat{J}^{2}=&J_{+}J_{-}+J_{z}^{2}-J_{z}\\
&\\
\end{array}
\]
and the number operator 
\[
\hat{N}=\sum_{p\sigma}
a_{p\sigma}^{\dagger}a_{p\sigma}.
\]

We will now rewrite the Hamiltonian in terms of the above quasi-spin operators and the number operator 
\begin{equation}
N = \sum_{p,\sigma} a_{p\sigma}^\dagger a_{p\sigma}.
label{eq:N}
\end{equation}
Going through each term of the Hamiltonian and using the expressions for the quasi-spin operators we obtain
\begin{equation}
H_0 = \varepsilon J_z, 
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
H_1 = \frac{1}{2} V \left( J_+^2 + J_-^2 \right),
\end{equation}
and 
\begin{equation}
H_2 = \frac{1}{2} W \left( -N + J_+ J_- + J_- J_+ \right).
\end{equation}

The above expressions can in turn be used to show that the Hamiltonian
commutes with the various quasi-spin operators. This leads to quantum
numbers which are conserved.  Let us first show that $[H,J^2]=0$,
which means that $J$ is a so-called *good* quantum number and that the
total spin is a conserved quantum number.

The matrix elements are given by $\langle J,J_z \vert H \vert J',J_z' \rangle$.
The
Hamiltonian is hermitian and we obtain after all this labor of ours

\begin{equation}
H_{J = 2} =
\begin{bmatrix}
-2\varepsilon & 0 & \sqrt{6}V & 0 & 0 \\
0 & -\varepsilon + 3W & 0 & 3V & 0 \\
\sqrt{6}V & 0 & 4W & 0 & \sqrt{6}V \\
0 & 3V & 0 & \varepsilon + 3W & 0 \\
0 & 0 & \sqrt{6}V & 0 & 2\varepsilon
\end{bmatrix}
label{eq:HJ=2}
\end{equation}





Its Hamiltonian can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
  \hat{H}_{\mathrm{LMG}} = -\frac{\lambda}{N} \left( \hat{S}_x^2 + \gamma\, \hat{S}_y^2 \right)
  - h\, \hat{S}_z,
  \label{eq:lmg-hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{S}_\alpha = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^N \hat{\sigma}_i^\alpha$ are collective spin operators,
$\lambda$ is the interaction strength, $\gamma$ the anisotropy parameter, and $h$ an external magnetic field.
The factor $1/N$ ensures a well-defined thermodynamic limit.
The total spin $\hat{\mathbf{S}}^2$ commutes with the Hamiltonian, implying conservation of total spin $S = N/2$,
and the system therefore lives in the symmetric subspace spanned by the Dicke states
\begin{equation}
  \ket{S, M} \equiv \ket{N/2, M}, \quad M = -S, \ldots, S.
\end{equation}




\subsection{Mean-Field and Quantum Phase Transition.}
In the thermodynamic limit ($N \to \infty$), a mean-field treatment becomes exact.
A semiclassical energy functional can be written in terms of collective spin variables
$(\theta, \phi)$ on the Bloch sphere as
\begin{equation}
  E(\theta, \phi) = -\frac{\lambda}{2}\sin^2\theta \left( \cos^2\phi + \gamma\, \sin^2\phi \right)
  - h\, \cos\theta.
\end{equation}
The ground state undergoes a second-order quantum phase transition at $h_c = \lambda(1+\gamma)/2$,
separating a symmetric phase ($\langle S_x \rangle = 0$) from a symmetry-broken phase ($\langle S_x \rangle \neq 0$).
Near this critical point, the entanglement entropy exhibits logarithmic scaling with system size, signalling a diverging correlation length in the collective degrees of freedom.

\subsection{Reduced Density Matrices and Entropy.}
Because of full permutation symmetry, the reduced density matrix of any single spin
is identical and can be obtained from the collective spin density operator $\rho$ as
\begin{equation}
  \rho_1 = \mathrm{Tr}_{2,\ldots,N}(\rho)
  = \frac{1}{2}\left( \mathbb{I} + \frac{2}{N}\langle \hat{\mathbf{S}} \rangle \cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma} \right).
\end{equation}
The single-spin entanglement (or \emph{one-tangle}) with the remainder of the system is quantified by the linear entropy
\begin{equation}
  \tau_1 = 4 \det(\rho_1) = 1 - \frac{4}{N^2} \langle \hat{\mathbf{S}} \rangle^2.
\end{equation}
This quantity vanishes in product states and reaches unity for maximally mixed single-spin states.

For bipartite entanglement between two subsystems of sizes $L$ and $N-L$, the von Neumann entropy
\begin{equation}
  S_L = - \mathrm{Tr}\, (\rho_L \log_2 \rho_L)
\end{equation}
is the standard measure.  In the symmetric Dicke basis, $\rho_L$ is block-diagonal and
its eigenvalues can be expressed analytically in terms of Clebsch--Gordan coefficients,
allowing explicit evaluation of $S_L$ for moderate $N$.

\subsection{Two-Spin Correlations and Concurrence.}
Pairwise entanglement between two spins is characterized by the \emph{concurrence} $C$,
defined from the two-spin reduced density matrix $\rho_{12}$ as
\begin{equation}
  C = \max \left\{ 0, \lambda_1 - \lambda_2 - \lambda_3 - \lambda_4 \right\},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_i$ are the square roots of the eigenvalues (in descending order)
of the matrix $\rho_{12} \tilde{\rho}_{12}$, with
$\tilde{\rho}_{12} = (\sigma_y \otimes \sigma_y)\, \rho_{12}^*\, (\sigma_y \otimes \sigma_y)$.
In the LMG model, due to full permutation symmetry, $C$ is identical for all spin pairs.
It reaches a maximum near the critical point and decays as $N^{-1/3}$ at criticality,
indicating that bipartite entanglement is sub-extensive even though the total
multipartite entanglement diverges.

\subsection{Multipartite and Mixed-State Measures.}
Beyond bipartite partitions, the \emph{global entanglement} and the
\emph{geometric measure of entanglement} are often used:
\begin{equation}
  E_{\mathrm{G}} = 1 - \max_{\ket{\phi_{\mathrm{sep}}}} |\langle \phi_{\mathrm{sep}} | \psi_0 \rangle|^2,
\end{equation}
where $\ket{\phi_{\mathrm{sep}}}$ is the closest separable state to the ground state $\ket{\psi_0}$.
For mixed or non-complementary partitions, the logarithmic negativity
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{N} = \log_2 \| \rho^{T_A} \|_1
\end{equation}
quantifies entanglement by means of the trace norm of the partially transposed density matrix $\rho^{T_A}$.

\subsection{Scaling and Universality.}
Near the quantum critical point, different entanglement measures show universal scaling with system size $N$:
\begin{equation}
  S_L \sim \frac{1}{6} \log_2 N + \mathrm{const}, \qquad
  \tau_1 \sim N^{-2/3}, \qquad
  C \sim N^{-1/3}.
\end{equation}
These exponents coincide with those predicted by conformal field theory
for one-dimensional critical systems, even though the LMG model is infinite-range.
Such universality underscores that entanglement, rather than spatial locality,
captures the essential signatures of quantum criticality.

### 🔹 1. What is a Parametric Matrix Model (PMM)?

A **Parametric Matrix Model** describes a system (or dataset) through a matrix whose **entries depend on a set of tunable parameters**. Formally, one writes:
[
M(\theta) = \sum_i \theta_i A_i,
]
where ( {A_i} ) are fixed basis matrices and ( {\theta_i} ) are parameters to be learned or optimized.
Such models can represent **Hamiltonians**, **covariance matrices**, **kernels**, or **linear transformations** whose structure reflects known physics, symmetries, or constraints.

---

### 🔹 2. Why this matters for machine learning

#### (a) **Compact, structured representations**

Unlike dense neural networks that learn arbitrary mappings, PMMs impose a **low-dimensional, interpretable structure**. This makes them ideal for:

* learning models with **few but physically meaningful parameters**,
* ensuring **stability** and **symmetry preservation** (e.g., Hermiticity, orthogonality),
* reducing overfitting and improving **generalization**.

#### (b) **Differentiable and compatible with gradient-based learning**

Because ( M(\theta) ) is parametric and smooth in its parameters, it can be integrated seamlessly with automatic differentiation frameworks (e.g., PyTorch, TensorFlow). This allows one to **train PMMs end-to-end** as part of a neural architecture—for instance, as a differentiable layer encoding physical interactions or correlations.

#### (c) **Learning from spectral or dynamical data**

In quantum mechanics, the spectrum and dynamics of a system depend on its Hamiltonian matrix. PMMs allow ML models to learn **maps from parameters to observables** (e.g., energies, eigenvectors, correlation functions). This has direct implications for:

* learning **effective Hamiltonians**,
* building **surrogate models** for quantum simulations,
* predicting **phase transitions** or **control parameters**.

#### (d) **Bridging data-driven and physics-informed modeling**

PMMs enable **hybrid learning**, where parts of the model are learned from data and parts are constrained by physical principles. For example:

* embedding PMMs in a neural network can encode **unitarity**, **time-reversal symmetry**, or **particle-number conservation**,
* combining PMMs with variational learning yields **interpretable latent spaces** corresponding to physically relevant degrees of freedom.

### 🔹 3. Concrete examples of PMMs in machine learning

| Domain                           | Example                                                                     | Role of PMM                                             |
| -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Quantum ML**                   | Parametric Hamiltonians used for variational quantum algorithms (VQE, QAOA) | Learn optimal control parameters or ground states       |

| **Control theory / RL**          | Parametric transition matrices                                              | Model and optimize stochastic dynamics                  |

This bridges **machine learning** and **quantum many-body physics**, allowing one to:

* infer **Hamiltonian landscapes**,

## **Parametric Matrix Models for Quantum Systems: A Physics-Informed Machine Learning Framework**

### **1. Scientific Background and Motivation**

The ability to model, predict, and control the behavior of complex quantum systems is central to the ongoing second quantum revolution. Across quantum chemistry, condensed matter, and quantum technology, the challenge lies in bridging the gap between microscopic Hamiltonian descriptions and emergent macroscopic phenomena. Traditional methods—such as exact diagonalization or coupled-cluster theory—are computationally demanding, often scaling exponentially with system size. Machine learning (ML) offers a complementary route by enabling data-driven discovery of patterns and effective models. However, standard ML approaches typically lack interpretability, stability, and physical consistency.

**Parametric Matrix Models (PMMs)** constitute a new class of models that combine the structure and interpretability of physics-based modeling with the flexibility of modern ML. In a PMM, physical observables or operators are represented as matrices whose elements depend smoothly on a set of tunable parameters:
[
M(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_i \theta_i A_i,
]
where (A_i) are fixed basis matrices and (\theta_i) are learnable parameters. This parametric representation preserves the linear-algebraic structure of quantum mechanics while allowing data-driven optimization. PMMs naturally encode physical constraints such as Hermiticity, particle-number conservation, or gauge invariance, ensuring that learned models remain physically meaningful.

Recent work by Cook, Jammooa, Hjorth-Jensen, D. D. Lee, and D. Lee (*Nat. Commun.* **16**, 5929 (2025)) demonstrated that PMMs can accurately learn effective Hamiltonians and emulate correlated quantum dynamics from data. This establishes PMMs as a promising framework for **interpretable machine learning of quantum systems**, enabling the discovery of emergent behaviors and controllable dynamics.

### **2. Objectives**

The overarching goal of this project is to develop a comprehensive theoretical and computational framework for **Parametric Matrix Models** and demonstrate their application to **quantum control and correlated materials**.

The specific objectives are:

1. **Theory development:** Establish a rigorous theoretical foundation for PMMs, including their representational power, symmetries, and connection to quantum field theory and control theory.
2. **Algorithmic implementation:** Develop efficient, differentiable algorithms for PMM training and optimization within TensorFlow and PyTorch, ensuring scalability to many-body systems.
3. **Applications to quantum control:** Apply PMMs to the identification and optimization of control Hamiltonians in mesoscopic systems such as superconducting qubits, optomechanical oscillators, and electrons on helium.
4. **Applications to quantum materials:** Use PMMs to learn effective low-dimensional Hamiltonians from *ab initio* or experimental data, capturing correlation effects and phase transitions in strongly interacting systems.
5. **Open software and data dissemination:** Develop an open-source PMM module as part of the Hylleraas Software Platform, ensuring reproducibility and uptake by the wider research community.

---

### **3. Research Plan and Methodology**

#### **3.1 Theoretical development**

We will derive the mathematical formalism of PMMs from the perspective of quantum information geometry and operator theory. The project will explore:

* The relationship between PMMs and variational quantum circuits (VQCs);
* Conditions for controllability and expressivity of matrix parametrizations;
* Theoretical guarantees on stability, convergence, and representational accuracy.
  This work will establish PMMs as a generalization of both variational and kernel methods with explicit quantum structure.

#### **3.2 Numerical and ML implementation**

We will implement PMMs as differentiable layers within established ML frameworks, using automatic differentiation to compute gradients of spectral and dynamical quantities. Efficient linear algebra backends will enable large-scale training of matrix models.
The team will explore different parameterizations, such as:

* **Low-rank PMMs** for scalable representation of many-body systems;
* **Symmetry-constrained PMMs** for spin, particle-number, and time-reversal invariance;
* **Dynamic PMMs** that incorporate time-dependent control fields.
  Model performance will be evaluated on synthetic and experimental datasets relevant to quantum control and materials science.

#### **3.3 Applications**

**(a) Quantum control.**
We will use PMMs to learn and optimize control Hamiltonians for mesoscopic quantum systems. Examples include:

* feedback and reservoir engineering in superconducting circuits (circuit QED),
* feedback cooling in optomechanical oscillators,
* electronic control of charge qubits on helium and defect-based spin qubits.
  By learning effective control landscapes directly from simulation or experimental data, PMMs can uncover optimal control strategies beyond analytical reach.

**(b) Quantum materials.**
PMMs will be trained to learn effective low-dimensional Hamiltonians from *ab initio* data or experimental observables (e.g., photoemission spectra, correlation functions). The approach will be applied to:

* the Hubbard and Heisenberg models for correlated electron systems;
* spin–orbit–coupled defects in silicon carbide;
* light–matter interaction in cavity QED materials.
  By parameterizing Hamiltonians in matrix form, PMMs can capture emergent phenomena such as Mott transitions or spin-charge separation.

#### **3.4 Implementation and collaboration**

The project will be led by **Prof. Morten Hjorth-Jensen** (UiO) with collaboration from **Prof. David Lee** (NCSU/UiO) and researchers at the Hylleraas Centre and the QONTROL initiative. Two PhD candidates and one postdoctoral fellow will contribute to theory, algorithm development, and applications. The project will leverage the national computing resources at Sigma2 and integrate its software within the Hylleraas Software Platform.

### **4. Expected Outcomes and Impact**

This project will deliver:

1. **A unified theoretical framework** for PMMs, establishing them as a key paradigm in physics-informed machine learning.
2. **Scalable computational tools** for learning and controlling quantum systems with guaranteed physical consistency.
3. **Demonstrations of PMM-based control and modeling** in realistic systems (superconducting qubits, quantum defects, and correlated materials).
4. **Open software and datasets**, enabling wide dissemination through the Hylleraas and QONTROL platforms.

The impact extends across physics, chemistry, and quantum technology:

* **Scientific impact:** PMMs provide a bridge between machine learning and analytical theory, offering interpretable insights into many-body correlations and control.

* **Educational and societal impact:** The project will train young researchers at the interface of physics and AI, aligning with national priorities in digitalization and quantum innovation.
